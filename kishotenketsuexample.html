The designer's notes

Kishōtenketsu is a four-part structure borrowed from Japanese storytelling. Game designers use it to teach a mechanic without ever writing "press A to jump." Here is what each section of this level is actually doing.

起 Ki — introduce, with nothing at stake

You meet the spring on flat ground. No gap under it, no drifter near it, no timer. There is a token floating above so you have a reason to bounce. If you fail, nothing happens, so you experiment.

In World 1-1, this is the first Goomba. It walks toward you on flat ground with a huge runway, so the game can teach "jump over things" without killing you for being slow.

承 Shō — develop, now it counts

Same spring, real consequence. One gap is too wide to clear on your own jump, and one ledge is too high. The spring is the only answer to both. You are not learning anything new here. You are proving the first section worked.

World 1-1 does this with pits and stacked Goombas. Same skill, higher price for missing.

転 Ten — twist, change the question

The drifters arrive. The spring still works exactly the same, but aim is no longer the hard part. Timing is. A twist does not replace the mechanic, it asks a different question about it.

World 1-1 drops the ceiling on you in the pipe section, so the same jump now has to fit under something.

結 Ketsu — conclude, everything at once

Spring, wide gap, height, and moving hazards, stacked into one run with nothing new added. If you clear it, the level has proof that you learned it. That is the whole point of the structure.

World 1-1 ends with the staircase and the flagpole. No new mechanic, just a clean test of the jump you have been practicing for ninety seconds.
